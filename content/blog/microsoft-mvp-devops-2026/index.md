<p>The email arrived this week: I have been accepted into the <a href="https://mvp.microsoft.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Microsoft Most Valuable Professional</a> program for the coming year, in <strong>DevOps</strong>. The award date is August 1, 2026.</p>

<p>I am genuinely happy about it, and I want to be clear about why, because the MVP award is one of those things that gets misread in both directions. It is not a certification. Nobody sits an exam. It is also not a job title, a partnership, or a discount code. It is Microsoft's way of saying that they noticed a year of unpaid, unglamorous community work and would like more of it, please.</p>

<p><strong>Twenty-one years of this</strong></p>

<p>I received the very first MVP award for Visual Studio Team System back in 2005, when Team Foundation Server was new, source control still meant a checkout lock to most people, and "DevOps" was not yet a word anyone said out loud. The product has been renamed roughly as many times as I have been renewed: Team System, Team Foundation Server, Visual Studio Online, Visual Studio Team Services, and finally Azure DevOps. The community around it never really changed. Same people, mostly, still trying to help teams ship software without losing their minds.</p>

<p>I <a href="/blog/how-do-microsoft-mvps-get-selected/">wrote about how MVPs get selected</a> back in 2006, and the honest answer has not moved much in twenty years. You do the work in public, consistently, for a long time, without knowing whether anyone at Microsoft is watching. Sometimes they are.</p>

<blockquote class="quote">The award is a lagging indicator. It measures what you already did, not what you are entitled to.</blockquote>

<p><strong>What this cycle looks like</strong></p>

<p>My contributions this past year clustered around three things, and I expect the next year to look similar.</p>

<p><em>Azure DevOps in practice.</em> Most of my writing is still about the unglamorous middle of the tool: workflow state categories, whether the Value Area field earns its keep, what a bug report actually needs in it, how one Product Backlog Item survives contact with nine teams. That work is not exciting and it is exactly what people are stuck on.</p>

<p><em>Open source.</em> Shipping code beats writing about code. The newest is a <a href="https://github.com/accentient/digitalai-agility-to-ado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Digital.ai Agility to Azure DevOps migrator</a> we are releasing under MIT this month, after using it to move 53,683 work items in a single run. More on that shortly.</p>

<p><em>Agentic AI and who owns what it writes.</em> The <a href="https://whoownsthecode.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">#WhoOwnsTheCode</a> initiative and the Claude Code course series both grew out of the same question teams keep asking me in the hallway. AI is now part of every DevOps conversation whether the tooling admits it or not.</p>

<p><strong>A small housekeeping note</strong></p>

<p>Starting with this post I am tagging community contributions with <a href="/blog/tags/mvp/">MVP</a>, so the work that counts toward the award is easy to find in one place. Partly that is for the annual submission, which is otherwise an archaeology exercise every June. Mostly it is so anyone who wants to see what "contribution" actually means in practice can just click the tag and scroll.</p>

<p>Thank you to everyone who read a post, filed an issue, argued with me in a class, or sent a question that turned into an article. That is the whole input to this. The award is just Microsoft noticing.</p>
